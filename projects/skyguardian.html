SkyGuardian

Real-time survivor detection from drone footage that briefs response teams on the terrain, hazards, and survivors ahead, before they arrive.

The problem

When disaster strikes, response teams usually know where to go. What they don’t know is what they’ll find on the way there, or when they arrive.

Without aerial footage, a team heads out with almost no picture of the ground: how many survivors are waiting, which roads are still passable, what is blocking them. So teams get stuck en route, running into rubble, collapsed roads, flooding, and other hazards nobody warned them about, and lose time that survivors may not have.

Drones can see all of this, but raw footage doesn’t help a team that is already on the road. Someone has to watch it, find the people in it, and turn it into something a responder can act on in seconds. And off-the-shelf detectors struggle here: they learned what people look like from eye level, not from directly above.

The approach

SkyGuardian turns aerial footage into a short briefing for the teams on their way: relevant images plus a concise summary of the scene.

Survivors: an estimated 4 people beside a partially collapsed two-story building

Obstacles: fallen debris blocks the main road from the north; downed power lines along the east side

Approach: the side street to the west appears clear; keep away from the leaning wall on the north face

  1. 1Drone videoUploaded to a FastAPI server
  2. 2Detect survivorsFine-tuned RF-DETR on every Nth frame
  3. 3Summarize the sceneQwen3-VL, once people stay in view for ~1 s
  4. 4Brief the teamOperator dashboard → rescuer app

The server streams annotated frames to an operator dashboard over WebSockets. When people are detected, a vision-language model returns a structured briefing: an estimated survivor count, a summary, the obstacles rescuers may face, and guidance on how to approach. The operator relays what matters to a rescuer app, which shares each rescuer’s location back every five seconds.

The detector runs behind one interface with three interchangeable backends: a Snapdragon NPU (TFLite through Qualcomm AI Hub), ONNX Runtime (preferring Qualcomm’s QNN provider, then CUDA, then CPU), or the plain RF-DETR Python package.

Key decisions

Fine-tune instead of slicing

The stock COCO-trained RF-DETR scored 0.00 mAP@50 on the Heridal aerial search-and-rescue dataset. Auditing its predictions on just 20 images showed why: it labeled what it saw as chair 1,461 times. Slicing images into tiles with SAHI, the usual fix for small objects, only reached 0.05, because the problem was how the model understood a person seen from above, not object size. Fine-tuning on Heridal fixed it.

Analyze only when it matters

Vision-language models are slow and expensive next to a detector. Rather than run one on every frame, SkyGuardian calls it only after people have been in view for about a second, then waits 10 seconds before calling it again. The detector does the constant work; the language model is saved for moments where context changes a decision.

One detector, many kinds of hardware

Disaster sites rarely have reliable connectivity. Keeping the detector behind a common interface let the same pipeline run on a Snapdragon NPU, a laptop GPU, or a plain CPU, switched with a single environment variable.

Separate apps for separate jobs

The operator needs the full picture: video, analysis, map. A rescuer on the ground needs short alerts and nothing else. Two focused apps sharing one WebSocket server kept each one simple.

What I’d do next

  • Measure the briefings. Compare the model’s scene summaries against human-written descriptions to catch counts or hazards it gets wrong.
  • Benchmark on-device, end to end. Time the full pipeline, from frame to briefing, on a Snapdragon device rather than component by component.
  • Stream live. Accept a live drone feed instead of uploaded video.
  • Put survivors on the map. Use drone GPS telemetry to place detections on the operator’s map.
