Penny

A personal finance agent that keeps track of every bill and subscription, plans your month with you, and tells you, in the moment, how much you can actually afford.

When
October 2026
Deployed on
Google Cloud Run
Data
Bank accounts through Plaid, shared expenses through Splitwise
Stack
Gemini (LiteLLM on Vertex AI), FastAPI, SQLite
Live demo (Columbia sign-in) → GitHub →

The problem

Money is spread everywhere. A paycheck lands in one account and everyday spending comes out of another. Rent is due sometime between the 1st and the 10th, the electric bill changes every month, a roommate covered dinner, and Splitwise says a friend paid you back, but you’re not sure they did. Subscriptions renew quietly in the background.

Keeping all of that in your head is exhausting, so most people don’t. Small purchases slip through because each one is too small to matter, until a month of them adds up to real money. And at the moment it counts, standing at the bar or about to check out, nothing tells you what you can actually afford.

Budgeting apps don’t fix this. They show charts of what already happened and leave the thinking to you.

The approach

Penny is the one place that does the thinking for you. You just ask, in your own words:

“I’m going out for drinks tonight. How much can I spend?”

“Splitwise says Priya paid me back. Did she actually?”

“Let’s plan October.”

  1. 1Sync your dataBank accounts (Plaid) and Splitwise
  2. 2Make sense of itLink transfers and splits, detect recurring payments
  3. 3Understand the questionGemini chooses from 14 tools
  4. 4Compute the answerDeterministic finance engine

An ingestion pipeline pulls in bank transactions and shared expenses, links transfers between your own accounts, matches Splitwise settle-ups to real deposits, and detects bills, subscriptions, and paychecks on its own. When you ask a question, Gemini decides which tools to call, and a finance engine written in plain Python computes every number: budgets, recommendations, shared balances, and what-if checks for planned purchases. Every tool call is shown alongside the answer.

Key decisions

Never let the model do math

Language models are fluent with numbers they’ve never checked, and in finance that’s unacceptable. Gemini never computes anything: it chooses tools and explains what they return. Tools hand back totals already added up, and the system prompt forbids stating a number no tool produced.

Find what the user never said

Penny only needs two facts from you: your rent and your monthly limit. It detects the rest, including subscriptions, utility shares, paycheck schedules, price increases, and memberships that stopped charging. Facts you give it outrank its guesses, and every bill is labeled Added by you or Found by Penny.

A budget that bends instead of breaking

Overspend on one category and a normal budget just turns red. Penny rebalances: bills come off the top, and what’s left is shared across categories in proportion to their remaining room, so the month still fits. That’s why “how much can I spend tonight?” changes as the month goes on, and Penny can explain exactly why.

Cross-check every source

Splitwise and the bank often disagree. Penny matches each settle-up to a real deposit, so it can tell recorded from received: “Splitwise says Priya paid you, but the money never arrived.” It also counts only your share of anything split.

Build for the way real data arrives

Bank data follows Plaid’s exact format, from cursor-based sync to pending charges that post a day later, and shared expenses follow Splitwise’s. The demo swaps out only the network call; everything after it is the production code path.

Ask before assuming

Plans are drafted from what you said, what Penny detected, and your history, then saved only once you agree. When a purchase fits no existing category, Penny suggests a new one and asks before creating it.

Results

The demo runs on Maya, a fictional grad student in New York with two bank accounts, roommates and friends on Splitwise, and a $2,925 monthly limit. Given only her rent and that limit, Penny found on its own:

  • Her subscriptions (Spotify, Hulu, iCloud) and her phone plan
  • Her share of the electric and internet bills
  • Her paycheck schedule
  • Spotify’s price increase
  • A gym membership that stopped charging in June

Detection re-runs on every sync, so what Penny knows always matches the data. The pipeline, detection, budget logic, and tools are covered by automated tests.

What I’d do next

  • Connect real accounts. Plaid Link with webhook-driven sync, and Splitwise through OAuth.
  • Move to a multi-user database. Postgres with a user on every row and encrypted tokens, in place of a SQLite file per session.
  • Remember across sessions. Make goals and preferences persistent per user.
  • Send reminders. Run the existing reminder rules as a scheduled job, delivered by push or email.
